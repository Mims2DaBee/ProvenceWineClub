Cassis Wine: An Australian Guide

Meet the small coastal appellation where white wine leads: Marsanne and Clairette, limestone terraces, seafood-friendly styles and the label clues that help Australians find the real thing.

By Emilie Blanc, Provence-born founder and editor · Editorial standards

In brief: Cassis is a protected wine appellation limited to the commune of Cassis, about 20 kilometres from Marseille. White wine is its signature and about 70% of production; Marsanne and Clairette are the principal grapes. The dry whites are known for floral, fruit, balsamic and sea-influenced saline or iodine-like notes. Cassis also makes rosé and a very small amount of red. Cassis wine is not crème de cassis.

What Cassis means on a wine label

Cassis is an appellation d'origine protégée (AOP), also seen as AOC in French usage. The protected name applies to still white, rosé and red wines that meet the appellation's rules. The grapes must come from the delimited area in the commune of Cassis in Bouches-du-Rhône; this is a precise origin, not a general coastal-Provence style.

The name creates an easy misunderstanding in English. Cassis wine is made from grapes and named for the Mediterranean town where its vineyards lie. It is not made from blackcurrants and is unrelated to crème de cassis, the sweet blackcurrant liqueur used in a Kir.

Cassis was recognised in 1936, among the earliest French appellations. That history does not make every bottle identical or automatically superior, but it explains why the compact vineyard has a clearly protected identity. Use the Provence appellation comparison to set it beside Côtes de Provence, Bandol, Palette and the region's other origins.

A limestone amphitheatre facing the sea

The Cassis vineyard sits east of Marseille in a natural amphitheatre of hills and cliffs open towards the Mediterranean. Vines are planted largely on terraces—restanques in Provence—within the commune. Limestone, the relief, sunshine and maritime influence all form part of the place, but none can be reduced to a single flavour.

Sea breezes can moderate the coastal environment, while surrounding relief gives Cassis a particular relationship with the mistral compared with more exposed Provençal sites. Growers still make the decisive choices: variety, parcel, harvest date, pressing, fermentation, vessel and ageing all shape the finished bottle.

“Mineral” and “saline” are common tasting words for Cassis white, but they are sensory descriptions rather than a claim that salt or stone passes directly into the wine. The current official European description is more careful: it calls the whites dry and round, with floral, fruit and balsamic aromas, and says proximity to the sea contributes an iodine-like character and lively impression approaching saltiness.

White is the signature, not the only colour

White

About 70% of production. Dry Marsanne–Clairette-led wines can combine white flowers, citrus or orchard fruit, herbs or resinous notes, rounded texture and a fresh, saline finish.

Rosé

About 28% of production. Grenache, Cinsault and Mourvèdre are central, with styles commonly described through red fruit and citrus. Judge the producer and bottle, not paleness alone.

Red

About 2% of production. Rare and easily overlooked, Cassis red can be structured and savoury, with red or dark fruit, herbs and undergrowth as it develops.

Those current proportions come from INAO's official Cassis profile and are useful orientation, not a promise about what an Australian retailer will stock. Imports are often intermittent and skew towards white. The appellation permits all three colours, while each producer decides what to make and export.

Marsanne, Clairette and the Cassis blend

Marsanne and Clairette are the principal white grapes. Marsanne can contribute body, perfume and texture; Clairette can bring freshness, floral character and a gently bitter or herbal edge. Their balance varies by estate and cuvée. The official rules also allow Bourboulenc, Pascal Blanc, Sauvignon Blanc, Terret Blanc and Ugni Blanc as accessory varieties.

The white blend is a useful distinction from much of wider Provence, where Rolle—the local name for Vermentino—is a familiar reference grape. Do not expect every Provence white to resemble Cassis, and do not assume a wine is Cassis because Marsanne appears on a retailer page: the protected place name must be present.

For rosé and red, the principal varieties are Cinsault, Grenache and Mourvèdre. Accessory grapes vary by colour under the specification. A permitted grape is not necessarily present in a particular bottle; use the producer's technical sheet for the actual blend.

How to read a Cassis wine label

  1. Find “Cassis”. It should appear as the protected appellation, commonly with AOP, AOC or Appellation Cassis Protégée/Contrôlée.
  2. Confirm the wine colour. Blanc, rosé and rouge mean white, rosé and red. An Australian listing may omit the French colour even when the bottle is clear.
  3. Identify producer, cuvée and vintage. These are more useful style clues than bottle shape, a coastal photograph or generic words such as “mineral”.
  4. Check the importer. A named Australian importer helps establish the bottle's route and gives a contact if the supplied vintage differs from the image.
  5. Avoid the blackcurrant trap. Search “Cassis AOP wine” or “Cassis blanc”, not cassis alone, which can return liqueur, syrup and fruit products.

Cassis is a small origin, so the label is likely to lead with the producer or cuvée as strongly as the appellation. The exact name still matters: “Provence white”, “Mediterranean white” and “Cassis style” do not establish protected Cassis origin.

How to find and choose Cassis in Australia

Start with the precise searches “Cassis blanc Australia”, “Cassis AOP wine” and “Cassis Marsanne Clairette”. Then search the producer or importer. Specialist French-wine retailers, independent bottle shops, importer catalogues and serious restaurant lists are more likely to carry it than a broad “Provence wine” category dominated by rosé.

Before buying, confirm producer, cuvée, vintage, bottle size and stock status. Product photographs are often carried over between vintages. Ask how the wine has been stored and how summer deliveries are handled; condition after its journey to Australia matters at least as much as a tasting-note adjective.

Cassis white can be enjoyable young for brightness and sea-food-friendly freshness, while some cuvées gain texture and complexity with bottle age. There is no universal drinking window. Follow producer guidance, consider the cuvée and storage history, and do not treat “latest vintage” as the only sign of quality. Serving Cassis wine with Australian food

Serve Cassis white cool rather than icy. Around 8–12°C is a practical starting range: lighter young wines can begin near the lower end, while a fuller or more mature cuvée may show more detail slightly warmer. Use a medium white-wine glass and let the wine change in the glass before deciding whether it needs colder service.

Classic coast

Bouillabaisse, grilled fish, shellfish, fish soup, aioli and fennel echo the appellation's Mediterranean setting.

Australian seafood

Try Sydney rock oysters, prawns, scallops, grilled snapper, flathead, crab or a simple seafood pasta with olive oil and herbs.

Vegetables and cheese

Try zucchini flowers, goat cheese, artichoke, roast fennel, white beans, almonds or herb-led vegetable dishes.

Match weight as well as flavour. A fine, young white suits simply dressed seafood; a broader, more mature or lees-textured wine can carry bouillabaisse, roast fish or richer sauces. Cassis rosé can suit prawns, tomato dishes and grilled chicken, while the rare red calls for producer-specific guidance and savoury food.

Quick answers

What is Cassis wine?

Cassis is a protected Provence wine appellation confined to the commune of Cassis near Marseille. It makes white, rosé and red wine, with dry white wine as its signature.

Is Cassis wine made from blackcurrants?

No. It is grape wine named for the town and appellation. Crème de cassis is a separate blackcurrant liqueur.

Which grapes are used in Cassis white?

Marsanne and Clairette are the principal varieties. Bourboulenc, Pascal Blanc, Sauvignon Blanc, Terret Blanc and Ugni Blanc are accessory varieties under the current specification.

What does Cassis white taste like?

The official profile describes dry, rounded wines with floral, fruit, balsamic and iodine-like notes and a lively impression approaching saltiness. Individual bottles vary with site, blend, vintage and producer.

Does Cassis only make white wine?

No. White represents about 70% of production, rosé about 28% and red about 2% in the current official profile.

How should Australians search for it?

Use “Cassis blanc”, “Cassis AOP wine” or “Cassis Marsanne Clairette”, then verify the producer, vintage, importer, storage and delivery conditions.
