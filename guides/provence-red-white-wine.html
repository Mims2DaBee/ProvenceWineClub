Beyond rosé

Provence Red and White Wine

The appellations, grapes and styles to know when you want to explore the other colours of Provence in Australia.

In brief: Provence is famous for rosé, but it also makes serious red and white wine. Start with Mourvèdre-led Bandol for structured red and Marsanne–Clairette-based Cassis for coastal white. Then explore Côtes de Provence, Palette and Les Baux-de-Provence. In Australia, search by appellation and importer because these wines are less widely stocked than rosé.

Rosé dominates the modern image of Provence, not the entire wine story. The same Mediterranean landscape also produces cellar-worthy reds, savoury medium-bodied blends and dry whites shaped by varieties such as Rolle, Clairette and Marsanne. They can be harder to find in Australia, which makes the place name on the label especially useful.

This guide separates broad style clues from legal facts. An appellation tells you where a wine comes from and which rules it follows; it does not guarantee one flavour. Producer, vineyard, vintage, blend, winemaking and bottle condition still matter.

What does Provence red wine taste like?

There is no single Provence red profile. Some wines are fresh, fragrant and made for earlier drinking; others are tannic, savoury and designed to develop. Grenache can bring warmth and red fruit, Syrah colour and spice, and Mourvèdre dark fruit, structure and an earthy or herbal dimension. Cinsault and Carignan may also appear, depending on the appellation.

Bandol is the clearest place to begin for structured red. Its identity is closely tied to Mourvèdre and patient élevage. The official INAO profile describes powerful, tannic wines with black fruit, liquorice and spice that can move towards truffle and undergrowth with age. That is a reference point, not a promise that every bottle should be opened late.

Côtes de Provence covers much more territory and a broader range of red styles. The official appellation profile distinguishes fruit-led wines from more complex, age-worthy examples that can show black fruit, cocoa and spice. Coteaux d'Aix-en-Provence likewise includes both robust reds and deliberately fresh, fruity, unoaked styles.

Red wine shortcut

Choose by intended shape, not colour alone. Ask whether the wine is fresh and fruit-led, savoury and medium-bodied, or structured for ageing. Then match the appellation, producer and vintage to that goal.

What does Provence white wine taste like?

Provence white wine is generally dry, but its character changes with place and blend. Rolle—the Provençal name for Vermentino—can bring citrus, pear, almond and fennel, with texture as well as freshness. Clairette can add floral and white-fruit notes; Marsanne brings body and perfume; Ugni Blanc can support freshness; and Sémillon or Grenache Blanc may contribute breadth depending on the appellation.

Cassis, on the Mediterranean coast near Marseille, is the essential white-wine name. INAO reports that white wine accounts for about 70% of its production and describes a Marsanne–Clairette base. The official profile characterises the whites as dry, with floral, balsamic and saline or iodine-like notes, and places them naturally beside local fish dishes.

Côtes de Provence whites are much less visible than its rosés but can be dry, citrus-led or floral. Rolle is an important clue. Palette, a tiny appellation near Aix-en-Provence, is notable for complex Clairette-led whites with recognised ageing potential. Les Baux-de-Provence whites commonly involve Clairette, Grenache Blanc and Vermentino and are described by INAO in terms of freshness, energy and stone fruit.

How to find Provence red and white wine in Australia

A search for “Provence wine” often returns a wall of rosé. Narrow the query to the protected place name and colour: “Bandol rouge”, “Cassis blanc”, “Palette rouge”, “Côtes de Provence blanc” or “Les Baux-de-Provence rouge”. Searching Rolle or Mourvèdre alongside the appellation can also surface specialist listings.

  • Search importer catalogues: a back-label importer can lead to other producers and colours from the same region.
  • Ask independent bottle shops: even when a wine is not listed online, a specialist may know the next shipment or a comparable appellation.
  • Check the supplied vintage: online product photos and titles can lag behind the bottle actually shipped.
  • Protect the wine from heat: choose careful delivery and avoid leaving bottles outdoors in Australian summer conditions.
  • Read beyond the front label: appellation, producer, cuvée, vintage and importer are more useful than bottle shape or marketing language.

Serving and food pairing

Serve Provence white wine cool, not frozen, so its aroma and texture remain visible. A fresh white may begin around 8–10°C; a fuller or more mature bottle can begin slightly warmer. Lighter reds can be refreshing with a gentle chill, while structured Bandol should be served cool enough to keep its shape rather than at a warm Australian room temperature.

  • Cassis or Rolle-led white: oysters, prawns, grilled snapper, scallops, bouillabaisse, fennel and goat cheese.
  • Fresh Provence red: charcuterie, roast chicken, tomato-based dishes, grilled vegetables and pizza with herbs.
  • Structured Bandol red: lamb shoulder, beef, duck, mushrooms, olives and slow-cooked dishes.
  • Palette or mature white: richer fish, poultry, creamy sauces and dishes with saffron or herbs.

Pair structure with structure. A delicate white can disappear beside a heavy sauce, while a firm young red may overwhelm simple seafood. When in doubt, choose the less powerful wine and let acidity connect the plate and glass.

Quick answers

Does Provence make red wine?

Yes. Bandol is the best-known reference for structured Mourvèdre-led red, while Côtes de Provence, Palette, Les Baux-de-Provence and other appellations also produce red wine.

What is the best-known white wine appellation in Provence?

Cassis is the clearest starting point because white wine is the majority of its production. It is not the only source: Côtes de Provence, Palette, Bandol and Les Baux-de-Provence also make white wine.

Can Australians buy Provence red and white wine?

Yes, but the range is smaller and less consistent than Provence rosé. Search by appellation, producer, grape and importer, and verify the current vintage before buying.

Is Rolle the same grape as Vermentino?

Yes. Rolle is the Provençal name for Vermentino.
